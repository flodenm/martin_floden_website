- Why do some countries grow faster than others?

We now extend the model in several steps:

1.  Allow labor input to grow

3.  Recognize that workers differ in human capital

4.  Add land and exhaustible natural resources

# The Solow model with population growth

## The basic Solow model

Recall the assumptions from Lecture 2: $$\begin{aligned}
        Y_t &= AK_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}, \\[2mm]
        K_{t+1} &= (1-\delta)K_t+sY_t.
    
\end{aligned}$$

Define output and capital per unit of labor: $$y_t\equiv\frac{Y_t}{L_t},
        \qquad
        k_t\equiv\frac{K_t}{L_t}.$$

The production function per unit of labor is $$
        y_t=Ak_t^\alpha.$$

## A growing labor force

Let labor input grow at the constant rate $n$: $$
        L_{t+1}=(1+n)L_t.$$

- We interpret $L_t$ as the number of workers

- We can also interpret it as population if employment rates and hours per worker are constant

- Population growth is exogenous: the model does not explain $n$

When $L$ grows, some investment is required simply to provide new workers with capital.

## Capital accumulation per worker

Divide the law of motion for capital by $L_t$: $$\frac{K_{t+1}}{L_t}
        =(1-\delta)\frac{K_t}{L_t}
        +s\frac{Y_t}{L_t}.$$

Since $$\frac{K_{t+1}}{L_t}
        =\frac{L_{t+1}}{L_t}\frac{K_{t+1}}{L_{t+1}}
        =(1+n)k_{t+1},$$ we obtain the exact law of motion $$
        (1+n)k_{t+1}=(1-\delta)k_t+sy_t.$$

## Investment and break-even investment

Rearranging the exact law of motion above gives $$
        k_{t+1}-k_t
        =\frac{sy_t-(\delta+n)k_t}{1+n}.$$

The direction of movement is therefore determined by the comparison between $$\underbrace{sy_t}_{\text{investment per worker}}
        \qquad\text{and}\qquad
        \underbrace{(\delta+n)k_t}_{\text{break-even investment}}.$$

- $\delta k_t$ replaces depreciated capital

- $nk_t$ provides capital for the expanding workforce

## Steady state with population growth

In steady state, $k_{t+1}=k_t=k^*$. Equation change_capital_per_worker then implies $$sy^*=(\delta+n)k^*.$$

Using $y^* = A(k^*)^\alpha$ and solving for steady-state capital per worker gives $$
        k^*
        =\left(\frac{sA}{\delta+n}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}.$$

Thus, $k^*$ is

- increasing in $s$ and $A$

- decreasing in $\delta$ and $n$

## Steady-state output per worker

Substitute the steady-state capital formula above into $y^*=A(k^*)^\alpha$: $$
        y^*
        =A^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
         \left(\frac{s}{\delta+n}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.$$

- Faster population growth lowers the steady-state level of output per worker

- It does so because more investment is needed to maintain a given capital stock per worker

- Population growth does not generate sustained growth in output per worker

## The Solow diagram with population growth

**Figure source:** `SolowDiagram.png`

**Figure description:** Solow diagram with capital per worker $k$ on the horizontal axis. Output $y$ and investment $sy$ are increasing and concave. Break-even investment $(\delta+n)k$ is linear. Their intersection determines steady-state $k^*$; below it investment exceeds break-even investment, while above it capital per worker falls.

A higher $n$ makes the break-even-investment line steeper.

## A higher population growth rate

Suppose the economy is initially in steady state and $n$ rises.

- Break-even investment rises immediately

- Investment is no longer sufficient to maintain the old $k^*$

- Capital and output per worker fall during the transition

- The economy approaches a new steady state with lower $k^*$ and $y^*$

A change in $n$ affects both the long-run level and the transitional growth rate of output per worker.

## Growth rates in the steady state

With constant TFP and population growth $n$:

| Variable               | Definition | Growth rate |
|:-----------------------|:-----------|:------------|
| Capital per worker     | $k=K/L$    | $0$         |
| Output per worker      | $y=Y/L$    | $0$         |
| Consumption per worker | $c=C/L$    | $0$         |
| Capital stock          | $K=kL$     | $n$         |
| Aggregate output       | $Y=yL$     | $n$         |
| Aggregate consumption  | $C=cL$     | $n$         |

The economy can have growing aggregate output even though living standards do not grow.

# Technological progress and balanced growth

## What can generate sustained growth?

Population growth allows aggregate output to grow, but output per worker is constant in the steady state.

Capital accumulation by itself cannot generate sustained growth in output per worker because capital has a diminishing marginal product.

But assume that TFP grows over time: $$
        A_{t+1}=(1+g_A)A_t.$$

The rate $g_A$ is exogenous: the Solow model now allows for technological progress but does not explain its source.

## A balanced growth path

Balanced growth path Along a balanced growth path, each variable grows at a constant rate (or is constant). Different variables need not grow at the same rate.

Recall that $$\frac{K_{t+1}-K_t}{K_t}
        =s\frac{Y_t}{K_t}-\delta.$$

The growth rate of capital can be constant only if $Y_t/K_t$ is constant. Output and capital must therefore grow at the same rate along a balanced growth path. Denote this common rate by $\gamma$: $$g_Y=g_K=\gamma.$$

Since $C_t=(1-s)Y_t$, consumption also grows at rate $\gamma$.

## Growth rates along the balanced growth path

Growth accounting for aggregate output gives $$
        g_Y = g_A+\alpha g_K+(1-\alpha)g_L.$$

Along the balanced growth path, $$g_Y=g_K=\gamma,
        \qquad
        g_L=n.$$

Substituting into the aggregate growth-accounting equation gives $$\gamma = g_A+\alpha\gamma+(1-\alpha)n,$$ and therefore $$
        \gamma =  n+\frac{g_A}{1-\alpha}.$$

## Growth per worker

Since $y=Y/L$, output per worker grows at the rate $$
        g_y =  g_Y-g_L
        = \frac{g_A}{1-\alpha}.$$

Capital and consumption per worker grow at the same rate: $$g_k=g_y=g_c = \frac{g_A}{1-\alpha}.$$

TFP growth raises output directly and also induces capital deepening. This is why output per worker grows faster than TFP along the balanced growth path.

## Steady state versus balanced growth

- A **steady state** refers to variables that are constant

- A **balanced growth path** allows variables to grow, but at constant rates

- With constant $A$, $k$ and $y$ are constant in steady state

- With growing $A$, $k$ and $y$ grow along the balanced growth path

In both cases, the capital-output ratio is constant in the long run.

## What the extended Solow model says

- A higher investment rate raises the long-run *level* of output per worker

- A lower population growth rate raises the long-run *level* of output per worker

- Changes in $s$ or $n$ generate growth during the transition

- Sustained growth in output per worker requires sustained TFP growth

The model explains how the economy responds to TFP growth, but not why TFP grows.

# Human capital

## Can physical capital explain income differences?

If countries have the same TFP, the production function predicts $$\frac{y_i}{y_{\mathrm{US}}}
        =
        \left(\frac{k_i}{k_{\mathrm{US}}}\right)^\alpha.$$

The parameter $\alpha$ is not free:

- Under competitive factor markets, $\alpha$ equals capital’s share of income

- The data suggest $\alpha\approx 1/3$

How much of the observed variation in output per worker can the model then explain?

## Physical capital explains too little

- With $\alpha=1/3$, the predicted relationship between capital and output per worker is too flat to account for the observed income differences

- A larger $\alpha$, such as $\alpha=2/3$, generates larger predicted income differences and fits the data better mechanically

- But $\alpha=2/3$ would imply that two-thirds of GDP is paid to physical capital, which is inconsistent with observed factor shares

Is there another input that behaves like capital but is compensated as labor?

## Human capital and effective labor

Let $h_t$ denote human capital per worker. Effective labor input is then $h_tL_t$.

Extend the production function to $$
        Y_t=A_tK_t^\alpha(h_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}.$$

Dividing by $L_t$ gives output per worker: $$
        y_t=A_tk_t^\alpha h_t^{1-\alpha}.$$

Human capital makes each worker more productive without changing the physical-capital share $\alpha$.

## From schooling to human capital

A simple human-capital production function is $$h=e^{\psi u},$$ where $u$ is years of schooling. If $\psi=0.10$, another year of schooling raises human capital by approximately 10 percent.

The Penn World Table uses the same idea, but allows the assumed return to decline with years of schooling: $$h=e^{\phi(u)}, \qquad
    \phi(u)=
    \begin{cases}
        0.134u,
            & u\leq 4,\\[1mm]
        0.134(4)+0.101(u-4),
            & 4<u\leq 8,\\[1mm]
        0.134(4)+0.101(4)+0.068(u-8),
            & u>8.
    \end{cases}$$

Thus, $\psi=0.10$ is a useful approximation, but PWT assumes lower returns to schooling beyond eight years.

## Years of schooling are not necessarily the same as skills

The PWT measure assumes that years of schooling can be translated into human capital. But it does not measure what students actually learn.

The recently released PISA 2025 results show substantial declines among Swedish 15-year-olds:

| Subject | PISA 2025 | Change since 2022 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Reading | 466 | -21 |
| Mathematics | 464 | -18 |
| Science | 485 | -8 |

Source: OECD, PISA 2025.

**If students spend the same number of years in school but acquire fewer skills, has human capital fallen?**

A measure based only on years of schooling will not capture changes in the quality of education. PISA concerns future entrants to the labor force rather than the human capital of today’s workers.

## Why did \alpha=2/3 fit better?

After adding human capital, production per worker is $$y=A k^{1/3}h^{2/3}.$$

Countries with more physical capital per worker also tend to have more human capital. If human capital is omitted, physical capital may therefore appear to explain both.

For illustration, suppose that $h=\sqrt{k}$. Then $$y
        =A k^{1/3}\left(k^{1/2}\right)^{2/3}
        =A k^{2/3}.$$

The better fit of the high-$\alpha$ curve does not mean that physical capital receives two-thirds of GDP. It may instead reflect that physical and human capital are positively related across countries.

# Development accounting

## Development accounting

**Development accounting** uses a production function to ask how much of the differences in GDP per person across countries can be attributed to differences in measured inputs.

For the empirical comparison, let $H$ denote total annual hours worked: $$Y=AK^\alpha(hH)^{1-\alpha}.$$

Dividing by population gives $$
        \frac{Y}{N}
        =
        A\left(\frac{K}{N}\right)^\alpha
        h^{1-\alpha}
        \left(\frac{H}{N}\right)^{1-\alpha}.$$

Here $H/N=(H/E)(E/N)$ combines hours per worker and employment per person.

## TFP as a development-accounting residual

Measure all variables relative to the United States. If productivity were the same in every country, measured inputs would imply $$\widehat{Y/N}
        =
        \left(\frac{K}{N}\right)^\alpha
        h^{1-\alpha}
        \left(\frac{H}{N}\right)^{1-\alpha}.$$

The productivity needed to match actual GDP per person is therefore $$\frac{A}{A_{\mathrm{US}}}
        =
        \frac{Y/N}{\widehat{Y/N}}.$$

- Technology and production methods

- Institutions and the allocation of resources

- Infrastructure and organizational capital not included in $K$

- Measurement error and omitted inputs

A residual is not, by itself, an explanation.

# Convergence

## Does the Solow model predict convergence?

Development accounting compares income levels at one date. The Solow model also has implications for how those differences evolve over time.

Consider economies with the same $s$, $n$, $\delta$, $A$, and human capital, but different initial levels of capital per worker. From the law of motion for capital per worker, $$
        \frac{k_{t+1}-k_t}{k_t}
        =\frac{sA k_t^{\alpha-1}-(\delta+n)}{1+n}.$$

Since $\alpha<1$, $k_t^{\alpha-1}$ is larger when $k_t$ is smaller.

The economy that starts with less capital per worker therefore grows faster as it approaches the common steady state.

## Absolute and conditional convergence

Absolute convergence Poorer countries grow faster than richer countries and catch up over time.

The Solow model predicts absolute convergence only if countries have the same steady state.

Conditional convergence A country grows faster when it starts further below its own steady state.

Countries can have different steady states because they differ in $s$, $n$, $\delta$, $A$, and human capital.

The examples show that an average convergence relationship hides very different country histories.

- South Korea, China, and Botswana experienced long periods of rapid catch-up

- Other countries stagnated or even became poorer over extended periods

## Interpreting the evidence on convergence

- Catch-up is visible in many individual growth miracles and is clearer on average among economies that are relatively similar, such as the OECD countries

- There is much less evidence of unconditional convergence across all countries over the full postwar period

- A low initial income level is not enough: a country may also have a low steady-state income level

- The empirical question is therefore whether countries converge after accounting for differences in steady-state determinants

Conditional convergence does not imply that all countries eventually have the same income per worker.

Let us focus on a narrower question:

Can technological progress and substitution allow output per person to keep growing as natural resources become scarcer?

## What if an input cannot be reproduced?

Physical capital can be rebuilt and expanded through investment. Other productive inputs are different:

- Land is in fixed supply

- Oil and minerals are depleted when they are used

We use the Solow framework as a stress test:

What happens to output per person when one productive input is fixed or gradually disappears?

## Land in fixed supply

Let $D$ denote a fixed quantity of land: $$
        Y_t=A_tK_t^\alpha D^\lambda
            L_t^{1-\alpha-\lambda},
        \qquad \alpha+\lambda<1.$$

Growth accounting gives $$g_Y = g_A+\alpha g_K+\lambda g_D
                   +(1-\alpha-\lambda)g_L.$$

Since $g_D=0$, $g_L=n$, and $g_Y=g_K=\gamma$ on a balanced growth path, $$
        g_{Y/L}=\gamma-n
        = \frac{g_A-\lambda n}{1-\alpha}.$$

## Interpreting the role of land

Equation growth_land shows that $$g_{Y/L} = \frac{g_A}{1-\alpha}
        -\frac{\lambda}{1-\alpha}n.$$

- Population growth reduces land per worker

- The growth drag is larger when land has a larger production share $\lambda$

- Output per worker can nevertheless grow if TFP growth is sufficiently rapid

A fixed factor limits growth but does not necessarily eliminate it.

## An exhaustible natural resource

Let $R_t$ be the remaining resource stock and let the flow used in production be $$X_t=uR_t,$$ where the extraction rate $u$ is constant. The stock develops according to $$R_{t+1}=(1-u)R_t.$$

Production is $$
        Y_t=A_tK_t^\alpha X_t^\beta
            L_t^{1-\alpha-\beta},
        \qquad \alpha+\beta<1.$$

Since $u$ is constant, the resource flow $X_t$ declines at rate $u$.

## Can growth continue as the resource is depleted?

Along a balanced growth path, $$g_Y=g_K=\gamma,
        \qquad g_L=n,
        \qquad g_X=-u.$$

Applying growth accounting to the resource-augmented production function gives $$
        g_{Y/L}=\gamma-n
        =
        \frac{g_A-\beta(n+u)}{1-\alpha}.$$

- Depletion and population growth are drags on output per worker

- Positive long-run growth is possible if TFP growth is sufficiently rapid

## From land to Malthus

Our analysis has treated population growth as exogenous.

Malthus instead combined

- land in fixed supply

- diminishing marginal product of labor, and

- population growth that rises with income.

In that setting, productivity improvements may raise population rather than long-run income per person.

We return briefly to this historical benchmark next time when asking why sustained growth in living standards began only relatively recently.

# Final comments

## What the Solow model can account for

The extended Solow model links output per worker to

- physical capital

- population growth

- human capital

- TFP, and

- fixed or exhaustible resources.

It predicts conditional convergence: economies grow faster when they are further below their own balanced growth paths.

## What remains unexplained?

Capital accumulation, education, and population growth help explain income levels and transitional growth.

Sustained growth in output per worker ultimately requires TFP growth in the Solow model. But the model does not explain

- why TFP grows

- why investment and education differ across countries, or

- why institutions support growth in some countries but not others.
