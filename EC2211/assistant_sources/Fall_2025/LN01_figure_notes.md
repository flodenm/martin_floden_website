# Lecture 1 figure notes — Fall 2025 archive

These notes describe figures referenced by
`LN01_Measuring_the_Macroeconomy.tex`. The image files are not assistant
sources. Descriptions report the visible content of the archived figures and
must not be treated as current statistics.

## What is GDP and what does it measure? / Do we produce more today or are prices just higher?

The chart shows Swedish GDP at current prices from 1970 through 2024, measured
in SEK billions. Nominal GDP rises from about SEK 196 billion in 1970 to about
SEK 6,392 billion in 2024. It motivates the distinction between nominal and
real GDP: the nominal rise can reflect both prices and quantities.

## Expenditure approach to Swedish GDP in 2023

The Statistics Sweden table reports 2023 GDP components at current prices.
GDP at market prices is SEK 6,143,187 million. The annotated rows map household
consumption to $C$, gross capital formation to investment, government
consumption and government fixed-capital formation to government purchases,
exports to $EX$, and imports to $IM$. The figure illustrates the accounting
identity $Y=C+I+G+EX-IM$ and warns that some government investment is recorded
inside gross capital formation.

## How do we compare “today” with “yesterday”?

Two stylized illustrations contrast a 1970s basket of communication and media
goods — rotary telephone, cassette recorder, television, and typewriter — with
a 2020s smartphone offering digital communication and streaming. They
illustrate the difficulty of comparing quantities and quality when goods
change or entirely new goods appear. They are conceptual illustrations, not
historical data.

## From 1970 to 2023 — linear scale

The chart indexes Swedish nominal GDP, the GDP deflator, population, and real
GDP per capita to 100 in 1970. By the end of the sample nominal GDP is above
3,000, the price index is around 1,000, real GDP per capita a little above 200,
and population around 130. On a linear scale the large rise in nominal GDP
visually dominates the other series.

## From 1970 to 2023 — ratio/log scale

The same four indexed Swedish series are displayed on a ratio scale with
evenly spaced proportional changes. Straight segments correspond to roughly
constant growth rates. The representation makes the persistent increases in
prices, population, and real GDP per capita easier to compare despite their
different total magnitudes.

## Very little growth before the 1800s / rapid growth thereafter

Long-run real GDP per capita for the United Kingdom, Sweden, Japan, and China is
shown on a ratio scale. Levels fluctuate around relatively low values for long
periods before accelerating strongly after industrialization; timing differs
across countries, with later rapid growth in Japan and especially China. The
figure illustrates that sustained modern growth is historically recent.

## World output shares

The chart shows approximate shares of world output for Asia, “the West,” and
the rest from 1500 into the early twenty-first century. Asia begins with the
largest share, loses substantial share during Western industrialization, and
then regains share late in the sample. The West follows the opposite broad
pattern, while the rest remains smaller. The categories are broad historical
aggregates rather than individual economies.

## Growth rates over time: United States, Germany, Spain, and Korea

GDP per capita is plotted on a ratio scale from about 1900 to the early 2020s.
Dashed trend lines show broadly similar long-run slopes near 2 percent annual
growth, while wars, depressions, reconstruction, and catch-up generate large
temporary deviations. Korea displays particularly rapid catch-up after the
mid-twentieth century.

## U.S. population, GDP, and GDP per capita

Ratio-scale series from the late nineteenth century to about 2020 show U.S.
total GDP growing at about 3.5 percent per year, GDP per capita at about 2.0
percent, and population at about 1.5 percent. The labels illustrate the
approximate accounting relation between growth in total output, output per
person, and population.

## Distribution of world population by GDP per capita

Two cumulative distributions compare 1960 and 2019, with GDP per capita shown
relative to the United States in 2019. The 2019 curve is shifted substantially
to the right of the 1960 curve, indicating higher income levels for much of the
world population. Large steps in the 2019 curve are labeled China and India,
reflecting their large population shares.

## Share of the world population living in extreme poverty

The plotted share falls from roughly 76 percent in 1820 to about 10 percent by
the late 2010s, with intermediate labels near 74 percent in 1850, 60 percent in
1900, 53 percent around 1950, and 25 percent around 1990. The figure illustrates
a long-run decline, with short-run fluctuations, in the archived series.

## GDP is not welfare, but …

The country scatter plot places GDP per capita on the horizontal axis and life
expectancy on the vertical axis. It shows a strong positive association,
especially at low and middle income levels, followed by a flatter relationship
at high income levels. The plot is correlational and does not by itself show
that GDP causes longer life expectancy.
