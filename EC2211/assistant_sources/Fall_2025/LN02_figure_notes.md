## Production and factor markets

### Diminishing marginal product of capital

The production curve is increasing and concave in capital while other inputs are fixed. Equal additions to capital produce progressively smaller additions to output. This is the graphical meaning of a diminishing marginal product of capital. The original slide cites Figure 4.1 in Jones (2024).

### Math repetition: profit maximization

A concave function reaches an interior maximum at `x*`, where the tangent is horizontal and `f'(x*) = 0`. The slide uses this to motivate the first-order conditions for a firm's profit-maximization problem.

### Equilibrium in the capital and labor markets

Two panels show downward-sloping factor-demand curves and vertical factor-supply curves. In the capital market the competitive rental rate equals the marginal product of capital; in the labor market the competitive real wage equals the marginal product of labor. The original slide cites Figure 4.2 in Jones (2024).

### Labor's share of GDP (`1-alpha`)

The U.S. labor share fluctuates over time and trends down in the later part of the sample. The figure illustrates that the Cobb–Douglas labor-share parameter is a useful approximation but not literally constant in the data. The original slide cites Figure 2.3 in Jones (2024).

### Capital's share of GDP, Sweden (`alpha`)

Several measures of Sweden's profit/capital share are plotted from roughly 1980 to the early 2020s. They move together but differ in level because the definitions differ. The broad message is that the capital share varies over time and rose near the end of the sample. The slide links to an Ekonomistas post drawing on Statistics Sweden and the National Institute of Economic Research.

## Development accounting and international capital flows

### Predicted per capita GDP relative to the U.S. if `y = k^(1/3)`

The table compares observed capital per person and GDP per person with GDP predicted from capital alone, using the U.S. as the reference economy. For many poorer countries, differences in capital alone predict much smaller income gaps than are actually observed. The lecture uses China as an example. The original slide cites Table 4.3 in Jones (2024).

### Predicted per capita GDP

The scatter plot puts actual GDP per capita on the horizontal axis and GDP per capita predicted from capital on the vertical axis. The 45-degree line marks equal actual and predicted values. Many lower-income countries lie above the line: capital alone would predict higher income than they actually have, pointing to productivity differences. The LaTeX caption identifies Penn World Table 10.01 as the data source and explains the construction.

### Much more differences in GDP per capita than in capital per capita

The World Bank comparison reports that physical capital per worker in middle-income economies is around 21 percent of the U.S. level, while GDP per worker is around 71 percent lower than in the United States. The intended conclusion is that capital scarcity alone cannot account for the full output gap. The original slide cites Figure O.2 in the World Development Report 2024.

### TFP needed to match observed income differences

The table infers each country's relative total factor productivity when the model is required to match observed GDP per person. The large inferred TFP gaps show why development accounting cannot treat capital as the only source of cross-country income differences. The original slide cites Table 4.4 in Jones (2024).

### Capital flows in the 2010s and 1980s

Both scatter plots relate the current-account balance (percent of GDP) to GDP per capita relative to the United States. A higher current-account balance means net lending abroad. The 2010–2019 plot shows some positive relationship, consistent with net capital flowing from richer toward poorer economies, but with substantial dispersion. The 1980–1989 relationship is more modest. The slide identifies IMF World Economic Outlook data.

## The Solow model

### The Solow diagram

Output per person is increasing and concave in capital per person. Investment, `sy`, inherits that shape, while depreciation, `delta k`, is a straight line through the origin. Their intersection is the steady-state capital stock `k*`. Below `k*`, investment exceeds depreciation and capital rises; above `k*`, depreciation exceeds investment and capital falls.

### The Solow diagram with output

The diagram adds output and consumption to the standard investment–depreciation graph. At the steady state, investment replaces depreciated capital and steady-state consumption is the vertical difference between output and investment.

### Exercise: Where is the steady state? (Poverty trap?)

The output and investment curves initially flatten, then steepen sharply over an intermediate range, and finally flatten again. They are therefore not globally concave. This non-concavity permits three positive intersections with the linear depreciation schedule: stable low- and high-capital steady states separated by an unstable threshold. Under unchanged parameters and without a shock large enough to cross that threshold, an economy below it converges to the low steady state. A sufficiently large capital injection or a parameter change can allow escape. The slide explicitly warns that this production function is not Cobb–Douglas.

### An increase in the investment rate

A higher investment rate shifts the investment curve upward. Capital and output per person then rise gradually toward a higher steady state. Growth is temporarily faster during the transition, but without continuing technological progress the long-run growth rate of output per person returns to zero. The original slide cites Figure 5.5 in Jones (2024).

### “Explaining” capital in the Solow model

The cross-country scatter plot compares actual capital–output ratios with the level predicted by the Solow steady-state expression using investment and population growth. The positive association supports some of the model's comparative-static prediction, though the data do not lie exactly on a 45-degree line. The original slide cites Figure 5.3 in Jones (2024).

### Growth rates in the OECD, 1960–2019

Initial GDP per person is plotted against subsequent average growth. Among OECD economies, initially poorer countries generally grew faster, illustrating conditional convergence within a relatively similar group. The original slide cites Figure 5.8 in Jones (2024).

### Growth rates around the world, 1960–2019

The same comparison for a broad world sample shows a much weaker convergence pattern and wide dispersion. Initial poverty alone does not guarantee rapid growth when countries differ in institutions, productivity, investment, population growth, and other fundamentals. The original slide cites Figure 5.9 in Jones (2024).

### South Korea and the Philippines, 1960–2019

The time-series comparison shows a sharp divergence: South Korea's investment rate and growth performance rose well above those of the Philippines. The case study illustrates how persistent differences in investment and productivity can generate very different development paths. The original slide cites Figure 5.10 in Jones (2024).
