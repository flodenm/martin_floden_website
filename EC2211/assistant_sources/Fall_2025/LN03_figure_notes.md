## The Solow model with population growth

### The Solow diagram with population growth

Output per worker, `y(k)`, is increasing and concave. Investment per worker, `s y(k)`, has the same shape. Break-even investment, `(delta + n) k`, is a straight line through the origin. Its positive intersection with `s y(k)` determines `k*`. A higher population growth rate steepens the break-even line and lowers steady-state capital and output per worker, holding other parameters fixed.

## Convergence

### Growth rates in the OECD, 1960–2019

The horizontal axis is GDP per capita in 1960 and the vertical axis is average per-capita GDP growth from 1960 to 2019. Among OECD economies, initially poorer countries generally grew faster. The negative relationship illustrates convergence within a relatively similar group. Ireland is a prominent high-growth observation. The original slide cites Figure 5.8 in Jones (2024), based on Penn World Table 10.0.

### Growth rates around the world, 1960–2019

The axes are the same as in the OECD figure, but the sample is much broader. Growth outcomes among initially poor countries range from rapid growth to stagnation or decline, so the convergence relationship is much weaker. The dispersion illustrates why convergence is conditional on investment, population growth, productivity, institutions, and other determinants of steady states. The original slide cites Figure 5.9 in Jones (2024), based on Penn World Table 10.0.

### Changing patterns of convergence

The two panels from Patel, Sandefur and Subramanian (2021) plot initial income per capita against subsequent average growth. For 1960–2000, the fitted relationship slopes upward, indicating no unconditional convergence over that period. For 2000–2019, it slopes downward, indicating that poorer countries grew faster on average. The grey bands are confidence intervals and the individual observations are labelled with country codes.

## Human capital

### Output in the data

The scatter plot shows a strong positive relationship between physical capital per capita on the horizontal axis and output per capita on the vertical axis across countries in 2019. It is descriptive and does not by itself establish causality. The data use 2017-dollar prices from Penn World Table 10.01, with capital stocks calculated as in Jones (2024).

### Output implied by `y = A_US k^(1/3)`

The observed country values are compared with output predicted from physical capital alone, setting productivity to the U.S. value and the capital exponent to one third. The predicted curve lies well above observed output for many countries with lower capital per person. With a common `A`, physical-capital differences therefore predict income gaps that are too small.

### Comparing `y = A_US k^(1/3)` and `y = A_US k^(2/3)`

The one-third curve remains above much of the observed data, while the two-thirds curve is closer to the cross-country relationship. The lecture then explains why simply setting the physical-capital exponent to two thirds is not satisfactory: it would imply an unrealistically high physical-capital income share and a labor share that is too low. This motivates adding human capital rather than reinterpreting the entire two-thirds exponent as physical capital.

### Rising return to education

For the United States from the early 1960s to 2020, the share of hours worked by college-educated workers rises strongly, from below 10 percent to above 70 percent. The college wage premium also rises overall, from roughly 30 percent to around 50 percent, though it levels off and falls slightly near the end. The simultaneous increase in relative supply and the wage premium is consistent with strong growth in relative demand for college-educated labor. The original slide cites Figure 7.8 in Jones (2024).

### Growth and inequality

The figure compares U.S. income growth across the income distribution over two 34-year periods. The 1980 profile slopes downward: lower and middle percentiles experienced the largest growth. The 2014 profile slopes upward and rises extremely sharply at the very top: the most affluent experienced the largest gains. The graph illustrates a major shift in how aggregate income growth was distributed. The original slide cites Figure 7.12 in Jones (2024), drawing on Piketty, Saez and Zucman.

## Malthusian growth

### The Malthusian model: evidence — plague and recovery

The graph traces combinations of population and a real-wage index in England from the late thirteenth to the early seventeenth century. The plague reduced population sharply in the fourteenth century and wages subsequently rose. As population recovered, wages fell; by around 1610, population and wages were close to their late-thirteenth-century levels. The movement is consistent with a fixed-land Malthusian mechanism. The slide uses material from CORE Econ.

### The Malthusian model: evidence — productivity changes

This version adds a subsistence-income locus and illustrates productivity increases in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Productivity improvements initially raise wages, after which population growth pushes wages back toward subsistence. The labelled trajectory highlights crop rotation, potatoes and the Chinese plough as examples associated with productivity gains. The slide uses material from CORE Econ.

### Malthus’ unfortunate timing

The time series shows real wages of laborers in England from about 1250 to 2000. Wages fluctuate without sustained growth for centuries, then begin a large and persistent rise after roughly 1800. The timing explains why Malthus's account fit much of earlier history but was published just as the long-run pattern was changing. The original slide cites Figure 1 in Steinsson.
