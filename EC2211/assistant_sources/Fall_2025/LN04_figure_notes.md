# Lecture 4 figure notes — Fall 2025 archive

These notes describe instructional figures referenced by
`LN04_TFP_and_Long_Term_Growth.tex`. The image files are not assistant sources.
Descriptions report only the visible content and the role of each archived
figure; empirical values must not be treated as current statistics.

## Rival and non-rival goods

The figure separates rival goods from non-rival goods and places examples
vertically by degree of control or excludability. Rival examples include a can
of soft drink (high excludability), a television, and fish in the deep oceans
(low excludability). Non-rival examples range from a coded satellite television
broadcast and a secret recipe through software and a GPS signal to basic R&D
and the Pythagorean theorem. The point is that rivalry and excludability are
distinct properties: an idea can be used simultaneously by many people even
when access to it can be controlled.

## A permanent rise in research productivity

Output per person is shown on a ratio scale. A permanent rise in research
productivity, $\zeta$, makes output grow temporarily faster than on the old
balanced growth path. The economy approaches a new path that is parallel to
the old one: the long-run growth rate returns to its previous value, but the
level of output per person is permanently higher. The figure labels this a
level effect.

## A permanent rise in the research share

The figure raises the fraction of workers in research, $\lambda$. At the time
of the change, fewer workers produce consumption goods, creating an immediate
negative effect on output per person. More researchers subsequently produce
ideas faster, so growth is temporarily higher. In the illustrated case output
eventually reaches a higher balanced growth path parallel to the old one. The
long-run growth rate is unchanged; the shown long-run effect is on the level.

## Total fertility rates

Two panels plot total fertility rates from about 1960 to the early 2020s. The
first covers Sweden, the United States, Germany, Italy, and Japan. Rates trend
down and are below replacement late in the sample, with Sweden and the United
States generally above Germany, Italy, and Japan. The second covers Korea,
China, Nigeria, India, and Egypt. All trend down, but from different starting
levels: Korea and China fall to especially low levels, India approaches about
two births per woman, Egypt remains higher, and Nigeria remains highest despite
a substantial decline. The figures motivate the implications of slower
population growth in Malthus, Solow, and Romer models; they do not establish a
causal explanation for fertility change.

## Growth accounting for the United States

The table reports average annual percentage growth and contributions for
1948–2021 and four subperiods. Over 1948–2021, output per hour grows by 2.4
percent per year: the contribution of capital per hour is 0.9 percentage
points, labor composition 0.2 points, and TFP 1.2 points (rounding explains the
0.1-point difference in the displayed sum). Output-per-hour growth is 3.3 in
1948–1973, 1.5 in 1973–1995, 3.2 in 1995–2003, and 1.8 in 2003–2021. The table
illustrates both the decomposition and variation in productivity growth across
subperiods.

## Components of long-run U.S. growth

The stylized accounting exercise decomposes 2 percentage points of annual U.S.
GDP-per-person growth since the 1950s into TFP (1.3 points), human capital per
person (0.5), the employment-population ratio (0.2), and the capital-output
ratio (0.0). A second pie decomposes the 1.3 points of TFP growth into research
intensity (0.7), population growth (0.3), and reduced misallocation (0.3).
These are an illustrative decomposition from Jones (2023), not structural
causal estimates or a forecast.
